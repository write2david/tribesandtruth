:::tip[Overview]{icon="sun"}
- The Twelve Tribes Communities teach that water baptism ("immersion") is a necessary step for salvation — the moment sins are forgiven and a person is joined to the body — not simply a testimony of a salvation already received.

- This view is called "baptismal regeneration." On this point, the Twelve Tribes agree with the Churches of Christ, the Roman Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Church, the Lutheran Church, and the Latter-day Saints.

## Response

- Baptism is not required for salvation. Salvation comes by grace, through faith, apart from works — including the work of being baptized.

- **Ephesians 2:8–9** — We are saved by grace through faith, "not a result of works, so that no one may boast."
- **Romans 3:22, 28** — Our righteousness comes through faith, apart from works of the law.
- **Romans 4:5** — Our righteousness is credited to the one who simply trusts God.
- **Titus 3:5** — God saved us "not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to his own mercy."
- **Romans 1:16** — The Gospel is "the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes." Belief is the stated condition.
- **John 3:16** — Belief, not baptism, is what is promised eternal life.

- Adding any work on top of faith — baptism included — reopens the door to the boasting Ephesians 2:9 rules out. If baptism were necessary for salvation, a person could point to their own obedience in submitting to it as part of what saved them. Paul's point is that salvation is arranged so that no one can do this.

- This doesn't make baptism unimportant. The New Testament assumes believers will be baptized (Matthew 28:19) as an act of obedience and public identification with Christ. The question here is narrower: is baptism a *precondition* of salvation, or a *response* to salvation already given through faith?

***

## Salvation Without Baptism in Scripture

Scripture repeatedly shows people saved, forgiven, or justified with no baptism involved:

- **The thief on the cross** (Luke 23:39–43). Jesus tells the dying criminal, "Today you will be with me in paradise." Baptism was not physically possible.
- **Abraham** (Genesis 15:6; Romans 4). Abraham was credited with righteousness through faith alone, centuries before Christian baptism existed. Paul treats Abraham's case as the pattern for how anyone is justified (Romans 4:23–24).
- **People Jesus forgave directly**, with no baptism in view: the paralytic (Matthew 9:1–7), the sinful woman (Luke 7:36–50, where Jesus says, "Your faith has saved you; go in peace," v. 50), the tax collector (Luke 18:9–14), Zacchaeus (Luke 19:1–9), and the woman caught in adultery (John 8:1–11).
- **Cornelius and his household** (Acts 10:44–48). The Holy Spirit falls on them *before* they are baptized, while Peter is still speaking. Peter later describes this as God giving them "the same gift" given to the apostles at the start (Acts 11:17), and receiving the Spirit is elsewhere tied directly to belonging to Christ (Romans 8:9). Their baptism afterward confirms a salvation already evident, rather than causing it.
- **The Philippian jailer** (Acts 16:31–33). Paul and Silas tell him, "Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved" — and only afterward, that same night, is he baptized.
- **Paul's own summary of the gospel** (1 Corinthians 15:1–4) is Christ's death, burial, and resurrection, received by faith. Baptism is not listed as part of the saving message.
- **Paul distinguishes his mission from baptizing** (1 Corinthians 1:17): "Christ did not send me to baptize but to preach the gospel." If baptism conveyed salvation, it is hard to explain an apostle treating it as secondary to his primary calling.

## Addressing the Key Prooftexts

A fair treatment of this question has to engage the verses baptismal-regeneration positions lean on most, not just present verses for one side.

### Acts 2:38 — "Repent and be baptized ... for the forgiveness of your sins"

The debate turns on the Greek preposition behind "for" (*eis*). It can mean "in order to obtain," but it can also mean "because of" or "with reference to" something already true. Two parallel uses of *eis* elsewhere with "repentance" or "baptism" show the word doesn't settle the question by itself:

- **Matthew 3:11** — John says he baptizes "for [*eis*] repentance." Baptism doesn't produce repentance; it responds to it.
- **Matthew 12:41 / Luke 11:32** — the Ninevites "repented at [*eis*] the preaching of Jonah" — repentance came *in response to* the preaching, not because the preaching manufactured it.

Read that way, Acts 2:38 fits the same pattern: baptism marks a forgiveness already occurring, rather than causing it. This also fits Acts 10:44–48, where the Spirit is given before baptism.

### Mark 16:16 — "Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved; whoever does not believe will be condemned"

Three points limit how much weight this verse can carry:

1. The condemnation clause names only unbelief — baptism is dropped from it entirely.
2. Most textual scholars consider Mark 16:9–20 a later addition, absent from the earliest Greek manuscripts, and so probably not part of the original text.
3. Even taken at face value, the verse doesn't say an unbaptized believer is condemned — only an unbeliever.

None of this argues against baptism, which the New Testament still commands (Matthew 28:19). It just means this verse doesn't establish baptism as necessary for salvation.

### 1 Peter 3:21 — "Baptism ... now saves you"

Peter qualifies his own statement in the very same verse: baptism saves "not as a removal of dirt from the body but as an appeal to God for a good conscience." He grounds the saving reality in "the resurrection of Jesus Christ," not in the water itself.

### Acts 22:16 — "Get up and be baptized, and wash away your sins, calling on His name"

The grammar is instructive: "be baptized" and "wash away" are both imperatives, while "arise" and "calling" are participles. That structure allows the verse to be read as, "Rising up, be baptized, and wash away your sins *by calling on His name*" — tying the forgiveness of sins to calling on Christ's name, not to the act of baptism. This matches Romans 10:13: "Whoever calls on the name of the Lord will be saved." (Not every English translation preserves this grammatical structure equally clearly.)

## A Note on the Greek Word "For" (*Eis*)

"For" is one of several possible English translations of *eis*, and it's a flexible word. Depending on context, it can mean:

- **Because of / with reference to** — e.g., Matthew 12:41; Luke 11:32.
- **In order to / so that** — e.g., James 3:3; 1 Peter 3:7.
- **Resulting in** — e.g., 1 Peter 1:7.

Context — not the word "for" by itself — has to decide which sense applies in any given verse. That's the real crux of the Acts 2:38 debate: both sides agree on the range of meanings *eis* can carry; they disagree on which one fits the context best.

## Conclusion

Salvation rests on faith in Christ, not on completing an ordinance — even one, like baptism, that Scripture still commands. A person's assurance shouldn't depend on whether they lived long enough to be baptized. It rests on Christ's finished work, received by faith alone.
